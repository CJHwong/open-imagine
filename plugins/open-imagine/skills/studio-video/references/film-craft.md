# Story, staging, editing and sound

Read before you develop a film or revise its story. These are practitioner methods adapted for small generative productions. Use [visual-language.md](visual-language.md) for shot and reference checks and [physical-continuity.md](physical-continuity.md) for world state. [connected-production.md](connected-production.md) carries each beat through to evidence.

## Develop the situation before coverage

Start from the producer brief. A raw premise supports several genres and viewpoints, so discuss that choice instead of imposing one. If feasibility calls for a different tone, premise or experience, take the alternative back to the producer.

Separate the producer's requirements from your directorial choices. Do not add restrictions the producer did not state, such as "no comic twist". Do not infer facts the brief does not give. A short needs no moral and no surprise ending. When the producer asks for a twist, build an earned change of understanding, not an appended event.

Write a dramatic sentence that includes the ending: who wants what now, what obstructs them, why this occasion matters, what their behavior contributes, and what they do when things change. Ordinary embarrassment is enough stakes. Recognition is not always an ending. Ask whether it leads to a decision, a recovery attempt or a changed relationship.

When the turn is open, compare a few different endings. For each, name the plants it needs, audience knowledge, emotional effect, causal link and production cost. Do not pick the most elaborate reveal because it sounds surprising. A plant works on first sight and gains meaning later. A second actor, an animal or a complex hand contact must earn its staging and generation cost. Alternatives stay proposals until the producer selects one.

For each beat, separate:

- What the audience knows and expects.
- What the character knows, wants and tries.
- The observable event that changes the situation.

If suspense depends on a goal, establish the goal before the opportunity disappears. Or share the character's ignorance on purpose and make the discovery matter. Repeating a cute clip or an anxious reaction restates the premise without escalating it. Repetition works when its meaning or pressure changes.

Separate a beat's information from its treatment. "Discovers the missed stop" is information. Searching for a familiar landmark, seeing it recede and lowering the phone is one treatment. Choose behavior for the intended experience. Make required plot scenes worth filming in their own right.

## Draft action and sound, then stage it

Write a timed action-and-sound script before you divide the runtime into shots. Each beat sustains pressure or changes the situation. Do not keep an old shot count or old assets at the cost of a better scene. A six-second cap is a ceiling, not a target. Noticing, hesitating, checking and deciding need different times. Do not fill a preset shot grid.

Stage cheaply first: a timed reading, stick figures, a floor sketch, or a chair-phone-bag blocking exercise. None needs rendered imagery. Find positions, entrances, sightlines, reachable objects, the action axis, light sources and the opportunity or obstruction. Save the result as the project's staging map and world-time timeline. In a portrait frame, relationships can sit on different depth planes.

Stage the competing attractions: what absorbs attention, what could interrupt it, why the character misses it, and what becomes possible or impossible. Choose cameras after the event is staged. Isolated close-ups cannot supply missing geography or conflict.

Design the set as an information system. Sightlines, obstructions, entrances and reachable props must permit the event and its coverage. Surface detail cannot repair unusable geography.

Give performance a task, not an emotion. "Read the station name, then find a way back before anyone notices" gives successive tasks. "Look more nervous" gives a symptom. Convert intentions into visible, testable actions for prompts. Keep face identity, emotional continuity and pose as separate checks.

The development package is a treatment with the ending and intended feeling, a timed action-and-sound script with plants and payoffs, the staging map and world-time timeline, and a framed storyboard with purposes, cut motivations, action phases and sound cues. Scale the detail to the film. These can share one document.

## Direct attention through cuts

For each cut, ask what the viewer waits to see just before it and what the next image changes. A glance leads to its target, an attempt to its obstruction, a warning to a listener, an ordinary detail to its changed meaning. Choose framing, distance, movement and lens for that relationship.

Match action phase, momentum, eyeline, orientation and focus where continuity is intended. A pose match can join two redundant images or preserve a wrong world. Dissolves, whip-pans and arbitrary angle changes cannot repair missing causality. Deliberate discontinuity needs a story reason and must respect the producer's continuity requirements.

Judge timing in real-time playback. Keep enough handles to cut on thought or movement instead of the clip boundary. Do not duplicate, reverse or crop footage to claim coverage.

Plan a visual trajectory when the story benefits: how framing, light, design and performance develop together and what stays stable. Tighter, darker or shakier is not better by itself. Establish an intended departure before you judge it as drift.

## Design sound with the scene

Plan sound while writing. An announcement over an absorbed face lets the audience notice what the character ignores. A door warning across a cut sustains one closing opportunity. Use J/L cuts when they express attention, reaction or continuity, not as decoration.

Record source, story-world status, perspective and timing for each sound. Plan the shift between objective environment and subjective attention without implying the world stopped. Keep ambience coherent through doors, departure and transit. Attach effects to visible events. Sound cannot repair a visibly frozen exterior or an impossible prop change.

Use [drama3-speech.md](drama3-speech.md) for performance markup, voice design, cloning and pronunciation. Voice identity, acting, lip sync and acoustic perspective each need review. A transcript confirms words, not acting.

Link each important cue to its beat and picture range. When recognition or a cut moves, reassess cue onset, perspective and mix. Keep dialogue, ambience, device playback, effects and music separately adjustable. Compare a purposeful music entry against sparse or no score. Continuous music and cutting on beats are not required.

## Select on evidence

After approval, test the hardest physical relationship and its return after a cut before an expensive batch. If it fails, change staging, references or technique. Batch independent approved work by loaded model, and wait for actual outputs before dependent continuations.

Review the assembled film at normal speed with sound for story, performance and rhythm. Watch it once without graphics, then with graphics and sound, and check that each layer clarifies. Watch muted to diagnose movement and contact. Listen without picture for timing, source, perspective and physical phase. Watch moving windows around cuts. Frame-step to locate a defect after you find it.

Record artifact, time range, defect, severity and next change. Distinguish pass for demo, revise, reject and unassessed. The producer approves exact versions. Do not accept a failed essential event to meet a timeline. Low resolution leaves identity detail, text and lips unassessable; carry those forward to finishing.

When audience feedback is available, ask without explaining the intent: what did you expect, what changed, when did you notice, how did it feel? Compare answers with the intended viewpoint. Record real answers. Never invent a comprehension pass. Retention data is ambiguous: a replay spike can mean interest or confusion.
